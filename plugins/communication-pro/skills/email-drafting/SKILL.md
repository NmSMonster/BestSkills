# Email Drafting

An email exists to produce an outcome — a decision, an action, an understanding — from a specific recipient, without damaging the relationship. Most bad emails fail because they bury the ask, mismatch the tone, or optimize for the writer's feelings over the reader's response. Write for how it will be *received*, not how it feels to send.

## Every email, before drafting

Answer three questions:
1. **What outcome do I need?** (A yes? An action by Friday? Just to inform?) If you can't name it, the email isn't ready.
2. **Who is the reader and what's their state?** Busy? Annoyed? Senior? What do they care about, what do they already know, what will their objection be?
3. **What's the relationship, and how must it survive this?** A one-off vendor vs. your manager vs. a peer you'll need next month — same content, different calibration.

## Universal structure

- **Subject line = the ask or the topic, specific.** "Decision needed: vendor choice by Thu" beats "Quick question." It sets expectation and gets opened.
- **Lead with the point (BLUF — bottom line up front).** First sentence: what you need or what this is about. Busy readers decide in the first line whether to act; don't make them dig for it.
- **Context second, only as much as needed.** Then details, then the specific ask with a clear action and deadline.
- **One primary ask per email.** Multiple asks → the reader does the easy one and drops the rest. If you truly need several, number them so none is missed.
- **Make the response easy.** Propose the answer, offer options ("Mon 2pm or Tue 10am?"), attach what's needed. Reduce the reader's effort to zero.
- **Short.** Most emails are twice as long as they should be. Cut throat-clearing ("I hope this finds you well, I wanted to reach out because…") — respect the reader's time and they'll respect your ask.

## The hard ones (where this skill earns its keep)

### Saying no / declining
Be clear, kind, and quick. Don't bury the no in so much cushioning the reader thinks it's a maybe. Structure: acknowledge the request → the no, plainly → a brief honest reason (not excuses) → an alternative if one exists. "I can't take this on by Friday. I could get it to you Wednesday next week, or Sam may have capacity sooner — want me to ask?" A clean no with a path beats a mushy maybe that wastes both sides' time.

### Pushing back / disagreeing (esp. upward)
Lead with shared goal, disagree on the path, bring evidence, stay curious. "We both want this shipped safely. I'm worried the current timeline skips load testing — here's the risk [specific]. Could we either add three days or cut scope X?" Frame as a problem to solve together, not a fight to win. Offer a way forward, never just an objection.

### Delivering bad news
Don't sit on it and don't sugarcoat it into confusion. Front-load the news, own it, then the plan. "The migration failed and we've rolled back; no data was lost. Here's what happened, what we're doing, and when I'll update you next." Clarity and a plan reduce panic; vagueness multiplies it. Take responsibility without groveling — one clear acknowledgment, then forward to the fix.

### Chasing / following up without nagging
Assume good faith, make it effortless to respond, add value or a reason. "Following up on the contract — did you get what you need from me? Happy to jump on a 10-min call if that's faster." Escalate gently over successive follow-ups (friendly → clear deadline → loop in a stakeholder if truly needed). Never passive-aggressive ("per my last email" reads as hostile); state the need plainly instead.

### Apologizing
One genuine acknowledgment of the specific thing, no defensiveness, then the fix and prevention. "You're right — I missed the deadline and that put you in a bind. I'm sorry. Here's the corrected report, and I've set a reminder so it won't recur." Over-apologizing dilutes it; excuses undercut it. Own it once, cleanly, and move to repair.

### Escalating
Escalate the problem, not the person. Facts, impact, what you've tried, and a specific ask. Loop in the right people transparently (no surprise cc's used as a weapon). "Flagging a blocker: X has been pending 6 days and is holding the launch. I've followed up twice [dates]. Can you help unblock, or advise who can?"

### Cold outreach
Make it about them, be specific, keep it tiny, one clear low-friction ask. Show you did your homework (a real reason you're writing *them*), state the value to *them* in one line, ask for one small next step. Generic mass-merge emails get deleted; a specific two-sentence one gets replies.

## Tone calibration

- Match formality to the reader and culture; when unsure, slightly more formal is safer than too casual.
- **Warm and direct beats cold or waffly.** You can be kind and unambiguous at once — that's the goal in every hard email.
- Read it as the *recipient* on a bad day: could any line be read as passive-aggressive, blaming, or curt? Adjust. Tone is what the reader perceives, not what you intended.
- Email is permanent and forwardable. Never write in anger — draft it, wait, reread. If it's genuinely fraught, a 10-minute call often beats ten emails; suggest that.

## Deliverable

Give the user the draft (subject + body), calibrated to the situation. If the situation is delicate, briefly note the choices you made (why this tone, why this structure) so they can adjust. Offer a shorter and a warmer variant when tone is the hard part. Flag anything that shouldn't be in writing at all (legal exposure, a conversation better had live).
