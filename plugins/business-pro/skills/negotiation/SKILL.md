# Negotiation

A negotiation is a joint problem-solving process where the outcome is bounded by each side's alternatives. You win not by out-talking the other side but by **preparing better**: knowing your walk-away, understanding their interests, and expanding the pie before dividing it. Most negotiations are lost in preparation, not at the table.

## The four concepts that decide outcomes

- **BATNA** (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement) — what you'll do if this deal fails. Your BATNA is your real power; a strong one lets you walk, a weak one means you need this deal. **Improve your BATNA before negotiating** (get another offer, another quote, another option) — it does more than any tactic. Know theirs too: how badly do *they* need this?
- **Reservation price** — your walk-away point, derived from your BATNA. Set it *before* the conversation, in writing, so emotion in the moment can't move it.
- **ZOPA** (Zone of Possible Agreement) — the overlap between your reservation price and theirs. If it exists, a deal is possible; the negotiation is about where in that zone you land. If it doesn't, no amount of skill makes a good deal — recognize it and walk.
- **Interests vs. positions** — a position is what they *say they want* ("€100k"); an interest is *why* (budget optics, internal parity, cash-flow timing). Positions clash; interests often reconcile. Trading on the dimensions each side values differently is how you both win.

## Step 1 — Prepare (where it's won)

Before any negotiation, work out:
1. **Your interests**, ranked — what you actually need vs. what's nice-to-have. Not just price: timing, terms, risk, relationship, precedent.
2. **Their interests** — put yourself in their seat: what do they need, what are they measured on, what are their constraints and pressures? Research it.
3. **Your BATNA and reservation price**, and an estimate of theirs.
4. **Your opening** — ambitious but justifiable (anchoring works; a well-reasoned aggressive-but-defensible first number pulls the outcome toward it). Have the *rationale* ready, not just the number.
5. **Your concession plan** — what you can give that's cheap to you but valuable to them (and vice versa — those trades expand the pie). Plan the sequence; never concede randomly.
6. **Multiple issues, not one.** A single-issue haggle is win-lose; adding issues (price + timeline + scope + terms) creates room to trade. Bundle.

## Step 2 — At the table

- **Anchor first when you're informed** — the first credible number frames the range. If they anchor extreme, don't counter near it (that legitimizes it); re-anchor with your own justified number and reset.
- **Ask questions and listen.** The side that understands the other's interests better controls the deal. "Help me understand what's driving that" surfaces the interest behind the position — and the flexibility.
- **Justify with objective criteria** — market rates, comparable deals, data, standards. "Here's what this role pays at comparable firms" is harder to argue with than "I want more." Fair-standard framing depersonalizes the conflict.
- **Trade, don't concede.** Every give gets a get: "I can do that timeline *if* we adjust the payment terms." Unilateral concessions train the other side to push and signal your number was soft.
- **Silence is a tool.** After making an offer, stop talking. The urge to fill silence with concessions is the amateur's tell.
- **Separate the people from the problem.** Be hard on the issue, soft on the person. Attacking the counterparty turns a deal into a fight; you usually want the relationship intact (and future deals).
- **Keep your BATNA visible to yourself.** Willingness to walk — real, not bluffed — is your strongest position. Deals improve the moment the other side believes you can leave.

## Step 3 — Close and protect

- **Confirm in writing** — restate the agreed terms clearly; ambiguity resolved later favors whoever's more aggressive. Get it documented.
- **Check the whole deal, not just the headline** — terms, contingencies, what happens if things go wrong. A great price with terrible terms is a bad deal.
- **Leave them able to say yes internally** — help the counterparty defend the deal to their boss/team. A win they can't sell internally falls apart.
- Preserve the relationship: a counterpart who feels beaten looks for an exit or revenge later. Aim for a deal both sides will honor.

## Specific situations

- **Salary/raise**: anchor on market data and your value delivered (not personal need); negotiate the whole package (base, bonus, equity, title, flexibility, review timing) — more issues, more trades. Your BATNA is another offer or your current stability. Never give a number first if you can get them to; if pressed, give a researched range with the floor being your real target.
- **Vendor/contract**: competition is your BATNA — get multiple quotes and let it be known. Negotiate terms (SLA, exit, price locks, liability), not just price. Volume/commitment for concessions.
- **Dispute/settlement**: BATNA is the cost/risk/time of not settling (litigation). Focus on interests (both usually want to avoid that cost); objective criteria and a face-saving frame get it done.

## Deliverable

A negotiation prep sheet: your interests (ranked), their likely interests, your BATNA and reservation price (and estimates of theirs), whether a ZOPA plausibly exists, your opening anchor with justification, your list of tradeable issues (cheap-to-give / valuable-to-them and vice versa), your concession sequence, and the objective criteria you'll cite. Plus the two or three lines to actually say at the key moments. If asked, role-play the counterpart's pushback so the user can rehearse.

## Rules

- Prepare BATNA and reservation price before talking — that's 80% of the outcome.
- Negotiate interests, not positions; add issues to trade rather than haggling one number.
- Trade every concession for something; never give unilaterally.
- Use objective standards and honest framing — persuasion, not manipulation or lies (deception wins battles and loses relationships, deals, and reputations).
- If there's no ZOPA, the skill is recognizing it and walking, not forcing a bad deal.
