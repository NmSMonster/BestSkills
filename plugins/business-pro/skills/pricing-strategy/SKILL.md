# Pricing Strategy

Pricing is the single highest-leverage lever in a business — a 1% price improvement typically beats a 1% cost cut or volume gain by a wide margin, and most businesses under-price out of fear. The job is to capture a fair share of the **value you create for the customer**, not to mark up your costs. Get the model and the anchor right; the exact number can be tested.

## Step 1 — Base price on value, not cost

Three ways to price; use them in this priority:
1. **Value-based (default, best)** — price against the quantified value to the customer. What does the customer *gain* (revenue, time saved, cost avoided, risk reduced) or *pay today* for the alternative? A tool that saves a team 10 hours/week at €50/hr is creating ~€26k/yr of value — pricing at €200/mo captures <10% of that and is almost certainly too cheap. Anchor to value, capture a fair slice.
2. **Competitor-based** — where the market sets expectations; use as a reference point and to position (premium / parity / penetration), not as the sole basis. Being "10% cheaper" is a race to the bottom unless cost is your actual moat.
3. **Cost-plus** — only as a *floor* (never sell below cost sustainably), never as the method. Cost tells you the minimum; it says nothing about what the customer will pay.

**Willingness-to-pay** is the real target. Estimate it: customer interviews (the Van Westendorp four-question method — "at what price too cheap / cheap / expensive / too expensive" — brackets the acceptable range), analysis of what they pay for substitutes, and actual sales tests. Segment it — different customers value the same thing very differently.

## Step 2 — Choose the model

Match the pricing model to how value accrues and how customers want to buy:
- **Subscription (recurring)** — for ongoing value; predictable revenue; the SaaS default. Monthly vs. annual (annual improves cash + retention; discount ~15–20% for the commitment).
- **Usage-based** — when value scales with consumption (API calls, compute, transactions); aligns price with value but makes revenue less predictable and bills less predictable for the customer. Often hybrid: a base + usage.
- **Tiered/seat-based** — scales with team size or feature needs.
- **One-time / perpetual** — for discrete deliverables; simpler but no recurring revenue.
- **Freemium / free trial** — a funnel, not a price: free tier must be genuinely useful yet leave a clear reason to upgrade. Watch that free users don't cost more than they convert.

## Step 3 — Package and tier

Good tiering sells more than a single price ever will:
- **Good-better-best (3 tiers)** works because most people pick the middle — design the middle as the one you want most customers on, and use the top tier to **anchor** (it makes the middle look reasonable and captures high-WTP buyers).
- **Fence the tiers on value metrics customers self-select by** — usage, seats, features that map to how much value they get — so heavier users naturally pay more.
- Put the features that high-value customers need in higher tiers; don't give away your differentiators in the cheapest tier.
- Avoid too many tiers (choice paralysis) or fences customers resent (crippling a core feature to force upgrades breeds churn).

## Step 4 — Psychology and presentation (real, use ethically)

- **Anchoring**: show the higher price/tier first; it frames everything below as reasonable. A premium option you rarely sell still lifts sales of the middle.
- **Charm pricing** (€49 vs €50) nudges perception for consumer/self-serve; for premium/enterprise, round numbers signal confidence — match the convention to the buyer.
- **Decoy effect**: a slightly-worse-value option can make your target tier obviously best.
- **Frame against value/time**: "€2/day" or "less than one support ticket" beats a raw annual number.
- Reduce sticker shock with the right unit (per seat, per month) — but never hide the total; deceptive pricing destroys trust and churns customers.

## Step 5 — Test and evolve

- Prices aren't set once. Test: A/B new-customer pricing, offer new tiers, run price increases on new cohorts first.
- **Raise prices deliberately** — most businesses under-price and fear increases more than customers resent them. Grandfather existing customers or phase increases; add/communicate value alongside. A well-run increase on a product delivering real value rarely dents demand as much as feared.
- Watch the metrics: conversion by tier, expansion/downgrade, churn vs. price, and whether discounting has become a habit (chronic discounting means the list price is wrong or the sales process is weak).

## Deliverable

A pricing recommendation with: the value case (what the customer gains, quantified), the chosen model and why, the tier/package structure with what fences each and the target tier, specific price points with the reasoning (and a tested range, not false precision), the anchoring/presentation approach, and a test plan to validate. Flag the biggest assumption (usually willingness-to-pay) and the cheapest way to test it before committing.

## Rules

- Anchor to customer value and willingness-to-pay; use cost only as a floor.
- Don't compete on price unless low cost is a genuine structural advantage.
- Design tiers around value metrics customers self-select by; make the middle tier the target and the top tier the anchor.
- Psychological techniques yes; deceptive pricing no — hidden fees and fake discounts win a sale and lose the customer.
- When in doubt, you're probably priced too low — test higher before assuming lower.
