# Business Plan

A business plan is a set of testable claims about how a venture makes money, plus the evidence so far. Its job is to expose the riskiest assumptions — not to hide them under adjectives. Write it so a skeptical reader can check every claim.

## Step 0 — Choose the right artifact

- **Lean canvas (1 page)** — idea stage, internal alignment, pre-validation. Default for new ideas.
- **Business case (3–5 pages)** — internal decision: new product line, expansion, buy-vs-build.
- **Full plan (10–25 pages)** — bank loan, grant applications, investors who require it, franchise/regulatory needs.

Confirm the audience with the user if unstated — a bank reads for downside protection, an investor for upside, an internal committee for strategic fit. The same venture reads differently to each.

## The load-bearing sections (any format)

**1. Problem & customer.** Who exactly has the problem, how painful is it (what do they do about it today, what does that cost them), and evidence it's real: interviews, waitlist, search volume, current spend on workarounds. *The plan's credibility is set here.*

**2. Solution & differentiation.** What you sell, why the customer picks it over every alternative *including doing nothing*. Name the unfair advantage honestly (distribution, data, team, cost structure, regulation) — "better UX" and "we care more" are not moats.

**3. Market.** TAM/SAM/SOM bottom-up with shown work (use the `market-analysis` skill for depth). One paragraph on why *now* — what changed to make this possible/urgent.

**4. Business model.** How money flows: pricing, unit economics (price − variable costs = contribution margin per unit; CAC; LTV; payback period), revenue type (one-off/recurring/usage/transactional). One clear sentence: *"We make money by ___."* If unit economics are negative at scale, the plan is a donation program — flag it.

**5. Go-to-market.** The first 10 customers by name/type, then the repeatable channel: what it costs, how it scales, evidence it works (tests run, benchmarks from comparable companies). "Viral growth" and "partnerships" without a signed partner are hopes, not channels.

**6. Competition.** Direct, indirect, and status-quo. A positioning table on the dimensions *customers* care about. Never write "no competition" — it means either no market or no research.

**7. Operations & team.** What must be true operationally (supply, hiring, licenses, key dependencies), who does what, and the 2–3 key hires with rough timing. For regulated fields: the specific permits and their lead times.

**8. Financials.** 3-year P&L projection (monthly year 1, quarterly after), cash-flow with the trough marked, break-even point, and funding need derived from the trough + buffer — not a round number. Every line links to a driver assumption (use the `financial-model` skill). Include a scenario table: base / slow (‑40% revenue) / fast.

**9. Risks & assumptions — the honesty section.** List the 3–5 assumptions that kill the venture if false, each with: current evidence, the cheapest test to validate it, and the mitigation. A plan without this section is a pitch, not a plan.

**10. Executive summary — write it last.** One page: problem, solution, market size, model, traction, team, ask. It must stand alone; most readers stop here.

## Quality bar (apply before delivering)

- [ ] Every number traces to a source or a stated assumption — no orphan figures.
- [ ] The riskiest assumption is named explicitly, not buried.
- [ ] Unit economics are computed, not asserted; CAC assumptions tied to a named channel.
- [ ] Competition section would survive the competitor reading it.
- [ ] A skeptic's three most likely objections are answered somewhere — pre-empt, don't hide.
- [ ] No filler superlatives ("revolutionary", "massive", "unique") — replace each with the specific fact or delete.
- [ ] Financials' totals actually add up across sections (cross-check ask vs. cash trough).

## Working method

1. Interview the user for the facts only they have (evidence, pricing, costs, team); research the rest (market, competitors, benchmarks) and label which is which.
2. Draft the lean canvas first even when the target is a full plan — it exposes holes in 30 minutes instead of after 20 pages.
3. Deliver the draft with **open questions and weak points marked inline** (`⚠ assumption — needs validation`), so the user sees exactly where the plan is soft. Sanding those marks off is their call, not a default.
